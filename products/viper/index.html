How to play

Four rules, zero tutorial screens. Viper is easy to pick up in ten seconds and hard to put down after your first near-miss.

  1. Press Start Game or hit any arrow key to wake the viper up. It begins moving to the right automatically.
  2. Steer with ↑ ↓ ← → or W A S D. On touch devices, swipe on the board or tap the on-screen pad.
  3. Eat the red food to grow one segment longer and add 10 points to your score. Food always respawns somewhere the snake isn't.
  4. Avoid the four walls and your own tail — either one ends the run instantly. Every five pieces of food, the board speeds up, so plan your turns early.

Built like a real game, not a demo

Under the hood, Viper runs on a proper grid-based simulation with a frame-driven render loop, so movement stays crisp no matter how fast your browser's refresh rate runs.

True grid movement

The snake occupies exact cells on a 20×20 board and turns are queued to the next grid step, so there's never any mushy diagonal drifting.

Food that always respawns

Every bite instantly relocates the food to a random open cell, so a fresh target is always waiting — never inside your own tail.

Escalating speed

Every fifth piece of food eaten shaves time off each step, ramping the challenge up gradually instead of throwing you in the deep end.

Persistent high score

Your best run is saved to localStorage automatically. Close the tab, restart your machine — your record is still waiting when you come back.

Pause and restart anytime

Life happens mid-run. Pause freezes the board instantly, and restart wipes the slate clean without a page reload.

Zero dependencies

Everything you see — canvas, styles, sound effects, and logic — lives in one HTML file. No frameworks, no build step, no CDN calls.

How the frame loop actually works

Viper separates two clocks: your browser's render clock and the game's grid clock. That's the difference between a snake that glides and one that stutters.

Every visual frame runs through requestAnimationFrame, which on most displays fires 60 or more times per second. But the snake shouldn't move 60 times a second — that would be unplayably fast. So each frame, Viper checks how much real time has passed since the last grid step. Only once that elapsed time crosses the current step interval does the snake actually advance one cell, eat, grow, or die. Everything else in that frame is just a redraw of the current state.

This is what makes the speed-up mechanic clean: increasing difficulty is just shortening that interval number, not touching the rendering code at all. It also means the game stays smooth and correctly paced whether your monitor refreshes at 60Hz, 120Hz, or 144Hz — the visuals redraw every frame, but the simulation itself is locked to real elapsed milliseconds, not frame count.

Strategy notes from long runs

A long snake is a liability as much as it is a trophy. These are the habits that separate a score in the double digits from one in the thousands.

Hug the edges deliberately

Perimeter laps give you predictable, repeatable paths with only one exposed side. Cut across the open middle only when the food is worth the risk.

Plan two turns ahead

By the time speed level 3 hits, reacting to the current cell is too slow. Decide your next two moves as soon as new food appears, not after you arrive.

Leave yourself an exit

Never trap your own head in a pocket with only one way out. A tight coil looks impressive but a single wrong turn ends the run instantly.

Your high score lives here, on your machine

There's no server, no login, and no leaderboard shared across the internet — and that's on purpose. Your best score belongs to your browser.

Every time a run ends, Viper compares your final score against the number saved in localStorage under a single key. If you beat it, the new value is written immediately, so it's already there the next time this page loads — even after closing the tab, restarting your browser, or turning your computer off overnight. Clear your browser data and the slate wipes clean, which is exactly what the reset button below does on demand, without touching anything else on the page.

Why build snake again?

Snake has been rebuilt thousands of times because it's the smallest possible game that still has real stakes: a single rule set, a single failure condition, and a score that only ever goes up until the moment it doesn't. Viper isn't trying to reinvent that formula — it's trying to execute it cleanly. That means grid movement that never feels mushy, a speed curve that ramps instead of spikes, and a persistence layer that respects your progress without asking for an email address. If you've played snake before, everything here will feel instantly familiar. If you haven't, the rules above will get you moving in under a minute.
